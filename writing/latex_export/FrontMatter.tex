\chapter*{Abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Abstract}

Software quality assurance teams routinely face a bottleneck that
automation has not yet resolved: authoring and maintaining the
end-to-end test scripts that verify application behaviour remains a
manual, time-consuming task requiring both programming skill and
detailed familiarity with a specific test-automation framework. This
dissertation investigates whether a domain-specific,
parameter-efficiently fine-tuned open-weight language model, embedded
inside an autonomous agentic correction loop, can close this gap by
translating natural-language user stories directly into syntactically
valid and semantically faithful test scripts, at a quality level
competitive with or exceeding much larger zero-shot commercial systems.

A curated dataset of 279 Jira-style user stories, each paired with an
independently authored Cypress and Playwright reference script, was
constructed and validated across sixteen functional categories and three
complexity tiers. Two commercial large language models, GPT-4o-mini and
Claude Haiku, were evaluated as zero-shot baselines. Two open-weight
models, Phi-3 Mini and Gemma 4 E4B, were adapted through QLoRA
parameter-efficient fine-tuning on Apple Silicon hardware into four
framework-isolated adapters, and embedded in a
Build--Measure--Assess--Decide (BMAD) agentic correction loop that
autonomously generates, statically inspects, scores, and iteratively
revises each candidate script against a composite quality threshold.

All eight resulting systems --- two baselines and two fine-tuned models,
each evaluated on both frameworks --- were compared on the complete
279-story held-out dataset using paired statistical testing, since every
system was scored against the identical set of stories. The results show
that fine-tuning produces a large and remarkably consistent improvement
in ground-truth semantic alignment: the ranking Gemma 4 E4B
\textgreater{} Phi-3 Mini \textgreater{} commercial baselines holds
without a single exception across both frameworks and across all sixteen
user-story categories, confirmed by ten paired Wilcoxon signed-rank
tests each significant at p \textless{} 10⁻²² and an overall analysis of
variance (F = 225.63, p = 1.01 × 10⁻²⁵³). This quality gain was found
not to come at the cost of syntactic reliability: on the complete
dataset, fine-tuned and baseline systems are statistically
indistinguishable in syntax validity (χ² = 0.00, p = 1.00). The
strongest configuration, Gemma 4 E4B on Cypress, reached a 100\% BMAD
acceptance rate across all 279 held-out stories.

Beyond the headline result, this dissertation makes two methodological
contributions of independent interest: the identification and correction
of two measurement artefacts during evaluation (an unstripped Markdown
code fence that had suppressed a baseline model's apparent
syntax-validity score, and an incomplete-sample effect that had
understated fine-tuned syntax reliability), and a category-level
analysis showing that residual gaps between fine-tuned and baseline
systems in certain categories are attributable to a known limitation of
lexical-overlap metrics such as ROUGE-L rather than to a genuine
capability gap. The dissertation concludes that agentic,
fine-tuning-based test-script generation is a practically viable and
statistically well-supported approach, and identifies execution-based
validation, human evaluation, and broader framework coverage as the most
consequential directions for future work.
